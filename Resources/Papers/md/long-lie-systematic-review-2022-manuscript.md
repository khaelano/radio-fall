## Abstract

           Background: The long term impacts of experiencing a ‘long lie’ following a fall in the older person
           are poorly understood. This systematic review explored the impact of a long lie fall on physical and
           clinical outcomes in older people requiring an ambulance.

           Methods: PRISMA guidelines were followed.

           Results: 70 studies were identified. Nine studies were suitable for full review. Four studies meeting
           the inclusion criteria were included. The Critical Appraisal Skill Programme (CASP) assessed the
           quality of all included studies. Three studies reported on people aged 65 years and older. One study
           reported on people aged over 90 years. Personal alarm use was examined in two studies. One study
           explored patient characteristics of people conﬁrmed to have fallen by paramedics at the scene. One
           study examined re-contact and characteristics of fallers referred to a falls prevention service.

           Discussion: Cognitive impairment and long lie were a caveat for falls and repeated falls. Personal
           alarm use was infrequent suggesting a need for supporting the older patient in appropriate alarm
           use and exploration of newer technologies to alleviate their need. Future research should focus on
           interventions for wearable, smart and e-technology for automatic fall detection and qualitative
           exploration of the lived experience.

   7
   8           clinical outcomes in older people requiring an ambulance.
   9
  10
  11           Methods: PRISMA guidelines were followed.
  12
  13
## 14           Results: 70 studies were identified. Nine studies were suitable for full review. Four studies meeting
  15
  16           the inclusion criteria were included. The Critical Appraisal Skill Programme (CASP) assessed the
  17           quality of all included studies. Three studies reported on people aged 65 years and older. One study
  18
  19           reported on people aged over 90 years. Personal alarm use was examined in two studies. One study
  20
  21           explored patient characteristics of people conﬁrmed to have fallen by paramedics at the scene. One
  22
  23           study examined re-contact and characteristics of fallers referred to a falls prevention service.
  24
  25
## 26           Discussion: Cognitive impairment and long lie were a caveat for falls and repeated falls. Personal
  27           alarm use was infrequent suggesting a need for supporting the older patient in appropriate alarm
  28
  29           use and exploration of newer technologies to alleviate their need. Future research should focus on
  30
  31           interventions for wearable, smart and e-technology for automatic fall detection and qualitative
  32
  33           exploration of the lived experience.

## 3   Introduction
 4
 5   Falls are a common occurrence and are the largest cause of hospital admission in the older person;
 6
 7   between 2017-2018 approximately 220,160 emergency hospital admissions in the United Kingdom
 8   (UK) were attributable to falls in this patient group (aged 65 years and over) with just over 66% of
 9
10   these patients aged over 80 years. 1 Hospital-related falls account for approximately 240,000
11
12   reported in-patient safety incidents in UK hospitals 2, with an estimated cost of 2.3 billion. 3 A fall is
13
14   defined as an event which causes a person to, unintentionally, rest on the ground or other lower
15
16
     level. For the purposes of this paper, a non-traumatic fall is defined as a fall not causing significant
17   trauma or injury to the patient. 4
18
19
## 20   The prevention and management of falls in older people have been the focus of UK national
21
22   government initiatives which have endeavoured to focus on the reduction of morbidity and mortality
23   in this patient group. 5, 6 The ambulance service is under growing pressure to provide care provision to
24
25   an increasing number of older people who fall in their own home 7; a frequent reason for an
26
27   emergency ambulance call. 8 Even in those people who do not necessitate hospital admission, there
28
29   is an increased likelihood of poorer health outcomes, an increased risk of future admission to hospital
30
     and increased morbidity than the general older person population. 9 Community related falls are
31
32   largely preventable, however, data suggests that approximately 1 in 3 adults over 65 will experience
33
34   one fall annually 3 and an estimated 5% of falls in the community result in hospitalisation for injuries
35
36   such as fractures. 10 Snooks et al 11 found that 8% of all calls made to the London Ambulance Service
37
38   were due to older people who had fallen at home, with 36% of these people not requiring an
39   admission to hospital, despite 49% of fallers requiring healthcare assistance in the two-weeks post-
40
41   fall, with an increased risk of death and hospital admission than the general population.
42
43
## 44   Clinical and physical age-related complications such as muscle weakness, visual impairment or loss of
45   balance, as well as environmental factors such as a hazardous environment increase fall-related risk.
46
## 47   Hazards in the home resulting in a fall cost the NHS in the UK approximately £435 million 12 and
48
49   adapting the home environment to improve safety and limit fall-risk has been a focus of interventions
50
51   in falls prevention. 13 Suffering a fall at home can be a precursor to further future falls and the
52
     rehabilitative resources required to manage an older person post-fall can place significant demands
53
54   on healthcare services. 3
55
56
## 57   Experiencing a long lie fall
58
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
     A potentially serious complication of community-related falls in older people is the inability to get up
 1
 2   following a fall, which is associated with severe psychological, emotional and physical distress
 3
     connected to a fear of future falls, pressure-related injuries and loss of mobility. 3 Providing
 4
 5   information on how to avoid a ‘long lie’, generally defined as being unable to get up from the floor for
 6
 7   over one hour following a fall, is a priority in the NICE recommendations for preventing falls in older
 8
 9   people. 3 However, there is scant evidence surrounding the long-term outcomes of people who
10
11   experience a long lie fall in the community setting. Fleming et al 14 undertook a prospective cohort
12   study in the UK to examine the use of call alarm systems and their association with experiencing a long
13
14   lie in people over 90 years of age who had fallen at home. In a sample of 110 participants, at one-year
15
16   follow-up 60% were reported to have experienced a fall on at least one occasion and falls in the
17
18   community setting accounted for 45% of all reported falls. Interestingly, just over half of all reported
19
     falls resulted in the participant being found on the floor, with the majority of these participants
20
21   requiring assistance to get up from the floor. A total of 15% of all reported falls resulted in a long lie,
22
23   however, accounting for the individual people who fell rather than the reported number of falls, long
24
25   lies represented 30% of falls. Those who did experience a long lie were more likely to suffer
26
27   subsequent serious injuries, admissions to hospital and future long term care. Conversely, Simpson et
28   al 15 explored the characteristics of older people who had fallen in Australia and called an ambulance
29
30   through a prospective cohort study from a population of 1,610 cases and found that just over 53% of
31
32   patients were still on the ﬂoor when the ambulance arrived. Due to the quick ambulance response
33
34   time, only 13% of these patients experienced a “long-lie”. Cannon et al 16 found no relationship
35
     between ambulance response time and long lie in a sample of 503 patients.
36
37
## 38   The physical and clinical impacts on the older person experiencing a long lie remain poorly understood
39
40   with variations in the patient population investigated, study methodologies and a lack of robust clinical
41
42
     evidence contributing to a lack of understanding around the long term impacts of a long lie fall. As the
43   ambulance service are required to respond to an increasing number of older people who fall,
44
45   understanding the physical and clinical impacts of a fall is fundamental to limiting the number of
46
47   people suffering a long lie.
48
49
## 50   Aims and objectives
51
## 52   This systematic review aimed to explore the relationship between a non-traumatic fall in people aged
53
54   65 years or over resulting in a long lie (defined as remaining on the floor for an hour or longer) and
55
56   subsequently cared for by the ambulance service, and the associated physical and clinical health
57
58   related outcomes. The following objectives were defined to achieve this aim:
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
         1. To determine if there is an association between length of time spent on the floor after falling
 1
 2           and physical and clinical outcomes for older patients.
 3
 4       2. To understand whether the type and timeliness of response provided by the ambulance
 5
 6           service has any physical or clinical consequences for older patients who have a long-lie after
 7
 8           falling.

## 18   Inclusion and exclusion criteria
19
20
     The population of interest were patients over 65 years of age who suffered a non-traumatic fall and

22
23   the ambulance service. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cohort, observational studies whether
24
25   prospective or retrospective in design, and audits were suitable for inclusion. No constraints were
26
27   placed on language of publication or date of publication. Conference papers, poster abstracts, opinion
28   papers and case studies were excluded.

## 12   Determinants of a long lie
13
## 14   Whilst all studies focused on long lie as an outcome, there were some differences in the measures
15
16
     used to examine the experience of a long lie. Fleming and Brayne 14 focused on participants inability

20

22

24   fallen by paramedics arriving at the scene. Scott 20 focused on post-fall contacts of people referred to
25
26   a falls prevention service with a specific focus on the outcomes of those with cognitive impairment
27
28   and exposed to long lies.

## 33   Ambulance response and patient outcomes
34
35
     A prospective observational study, in New South Wales, Australia, including people aged 65 years and
36   older who received an emergency response and were conﬁrmed to have fallen by paramedics at the
37
38   scene was reported by Simpson. 15 Fall-specific information, ambulance clinical records and computer-
39
40   aided dispatch data was collected, with an emergency call system ambulance response deﬁned as
41
42   priority of 1 (urgent) or 2 (non-urgent). Those incidents allocated a response priority of 3–9 (cases not
43   originating via the emergency call system including routine transports, operational standbys, medical
44
45   appointments, and inter hospital transfers) were excluded. Over a 9-month period, 1,720 cases were
46
47   submitted with the ﬁnal sample comprising of 1,610 patient cases with a mean age of 83(8) and 61%
48
49   of patients being female. The results demonstrated a significant relationship between increased age
50
51
     and suffering a fall (p = 0.002) with 44% (n=712) of fallers never having previously called an ambulance
52   due to a fall, whilst 15% (n=248) had required an ambulance in the past month. The majority of falls
53
54   occurred in a community dwelling (n=1018) with the most common location being the bedroom (23%)
55
56   and living/dining room (20%). Eighty percent of falls (1,274/1,610) were from a standing height,
57
58   followed by falls from bed and on stairs (each 7%) with the most commonly reported reason for falling
59   being “loss of balance” (30%) or a “trip” (25%). In just over half of the cases the patient was still on
60
61
62
63
64
65
     the floor when the ambulance arrived (53%) with 13% of these patients experiencing a long lie. The
 1
 2   authors concluded trips decreased and loss of balance increased as age group increased.
 3

## 5   Patient outcomes were also the focus of Scott 20 who focused on the characteristics, specifically
 6
 7   cognitive impairment and long lie, and epidemiology of patients who re-contact the ambulance service
 8
 9   in the subsequent 6-months following referral to a falls prevention service due to an ambulance
10
11   response for a fall. Using a retrospective cross-sectional cohort design, the study identified 1079 calls
12   to the ambulance service for people aged ≥ 65 years and over for a fall, with 7% (n=75) of these fallers
13
14   resulting in a referral to a falls prevention service. A total of 136 re-contacts were made, with data on
15
16   reasons for repeat contacts being available for 101 of these individuals. Repeat falls were responsible
17
18   for 58% (n=59) of re-contacts. Long lies occurred in 81.2% (n = 56) of cases and 78.2% (n = 18) of
19
     patients exposed to a long lie re-contacted the service at least once within 6 months. Twenty patients
20
21   re-contacted due to cognitive impairment.

## 25   Fleming and Brayne 14 reported data following a 1 year prospective cohort study of ageing, using fall
26
27   calendars, phone calls, and visits in the community and care homes in in one city in England.
## 28   Participants included 90 women and 20 men aged over 90 (n=110), who were the surviving
29
30   participants of the Cambridge City over-75s Cohort. The study focused on participants inability to get
31
32   up without help, lying on floor for a long time after falling, associated factors; availability and use of
33
34   call alarm systems; participants’views on using call alarms to summon help if needed after falling. In
35
     this study, the authors found that call alarm systems were widely available but were often not used
36
37   and almost all patients of those who lay on the floor for a long time and had a call alarm system did
38
39   not activate it (97%). Lying on the floor for over an hour was strongly associated with serious injuries,
40
41   admissions to hospital, and subsequent moves into long term care.

## 46   Presence and impact of alarm use
47
## 48   Johnston et al. 19 examined the differences in fall characteristics and emergency service response to
49
50   older fallers (≥ 65 years of age), considering their use, or not, of a personal alarm using a retrospective
51
52   one-month audit of South Australian Ambulance Service (SAAS) records. Data on two samples of fallers
53
54   were obtained consisting of cases between the specified study period coded as “falls” or “alarm
55   activations” or “alert button activation error” (sample 1, n=1700) and a second sample which excluded
56
57   alarm activation errors (n=224) but included those people ≥ 65 years of age who used a personal alarm
58
59   to call the ambulance (n=124), in order to determine the scope of ambulance call outs for all falls and
60
61
62
63
64
65
     alarm activations. The data demonstrated that in Sample 1, 379 of 1700 callers used a personal alarm
 1
 2   to call the ambulance, with 58% of these alarm-activated calls being false alarms (neither lift nor
 3
     hospital transport service provided). In Sample 2 the majority of alarm calls were made by females
 4
 5   (72%) who were alone at the time of fall (78%). Long lies (> one hour) were reported in 11 cases of
 6
 7   non-alarm users and a further 13 cases had an unspecified length of time on the floor following a fall
 8
 9   before the ambulance arrived. The authors concluded that personal alarms should not be considered
10
11   a reliable preventative measure for avoiding a long lie.
12
13
14   In their community based research, Fleming and Brayne 14 identified that call alarm systems were
15
16   widely available but were often not used by participants, despite their data revealing virtually no one
17
18   who did use an alarm lay more than an hour on the floor, yet nearly all (97%) of those who lay on the
19
     floor for a long time and had a call alarm system did not activate it.
20
21
22
23
24
     Prevalence of long lie
25
## 26   The studies included in this review identified that long lies were relatively uncommon and found
27
28   limited evidence on the physical and clinical impacts of older people experiencing a long lie and
29
30   requiring an ambulance response. In a sample of 110 patients, Fleming and Brayne 14 found that
31   laying on the floor for over an hour was strongly associated with serious injuries, admissions to
32
33   hospital, and subsequent moves into long term care. In a sample of patients who did or did not use
34
35   their personal alarm to call the ambulance (n=124), Johnston et al. 19 found long lies were reported
36
37   in 11 cases of non-alarm users and a further 13 cases had an unspecified length of time on the floor
38
     following a fall before the ambulance arrived. Scott 19 found that long lies were documented for 56
39
40   out of a total of 75 service users requiring an ambulance following a fall and 41.1% of these patients
41
42   experienced a long lie. The study by Simpson 15 found that from a sample of 1610 patient cases, 13%
43
44   of the 53% of patients who were still on the floor when the ambulance arrived experienced a long
45
46   lie.
47

## Discussion
53
     This review synthesised evidence exploring the relationship between a non-traumatic fall in the
54
55   older person resulting in a long lie and poorer physical and clinical health outcomes. Limited
56
57   evidence was found surrounding the long term outcomes of long lies with only four cohort studies
58
59   meeting the inclusion criteria. Two studies specifically reported the effect of long lies on health
60
61
62
63
64
65
     outcomes. 14 19 The quality of all included studies was assessed using appropriate appraisal checklist
 1
 2   developed by the Critical Appraisal Skill Programme (CASP). 18 All four cohort studies were subjected
 3
     to quality assessment via risk of bias evaluation and had a low or very low risk of bias with all studies
 4
 5   focusing on long lie as an outcome.
 6
 7
 8
 9
## 10   All studies reported that the majority of falls in the community occurred in the home setting, with the
11
12   person predominantly being found alone when the ambulance service arrived. Only a small number
13
14   of ambulance calls following a fall resulted in a long lie, attributed to the rapid response times of the
15
16   ambulance service, which prevented the manifestation of fall-related complications. Transport to
17   hospital for fall-related injuries were relatively uncommon, which is consistent with previous research.
18
     11
## 19        All included studies suffered from the methodological limitation of a reliance on faller recall or
20
21   clinical documentation to determine the amount of time the person had been on the floor prior to
22
23   calling the ambulance, meaning it is difficult to establish the exact length of time between
24
     experiencing a fall and receiving assistance.
25
26
27   Whilst a significant proportion of calls to the ambulance do not result in traumatic injury to the patient,
28
29   the evidence suggests that there is a relationship between falls in the community setting and
30
     increased future morbidity, with many people necessitating future fall-related healthcare assistance
31
32   and support. Indeed, Flemming and Brayne 14 found that serious injury was consistently associated
33
34   with lying on the floor above one hour with 60% of those who were found on the floor following
35
36   ambulance assistance subsequently admitted to hospital with a fall-related issue. Furthermore, in this
37
38   study, long lies were associated with subsequent falls and future moves into long-term care
39   demonstrating the potential long-term future health implications that experiencing a long lie may
40
41   entail. Scott 20 also found that patients experiencing a long lie were also more likely to require future
42
43   fall-related ambulance assistance.
44
## 45   Although not a focus of this review, the finding that personal alarm use was minimal in two studies is
46
47   an interesting area of exploration. The reluctance to activate an alarm call from fallers found by
48
## 49   Flemming and Brayne 14 could be consistent with evidence demonstrating that the non-use of alarms
50
51   is associated with being unable to access the alarm, perceived patient burden and potential
52
53
     consequences of alarm activation. 19 Although alarm use should not necessarily be considered a
54   preventative measure for falling or long lie 14 some evidence points towards their value in supporting
55
56   people who experience a fall and require help. 21 It appears that the hesitancy of alarm use is
57
58   concomitant with fear and anxiety around their usage and suggests that there is a need for teaching
59
60   and support for the older patient in activating the alarm and supporting older people through
61
62
63
64
65
     education on the effective and appropriate use of personal alarm systems could prove valuable in
 1
 2   limiting the effects of experiencing a long lie. Newer technologies to alleviate the need for falls victims
 3
     to personally trigger alarms, or the potential for big dataset interrogations to better describe health
 4
 5   outcomes.
 6
 7
## 8   Future complications as a result of experiencing a long lie is not a new phenomenon; Wild et al. 22
 9
10
     found that older people experiencing a long lie were five times more likely to die within one year than
11   age and sex matched controls, indicative of the vulnerability a long lie can have on ill-health. In a
12
13   community based cohort study examining the predictors and prognosis associated with the inability
14
15   to get up following a fall in people aged 72 years and over, Tinetti et al. 23 found that being unable to
16
17   get up was associated with an increased risk of future hospitalisation, reduced mobility and an
18   increased risk of mortality than those fallers who could get up, although these findings were not
19
20   significant. Although there appears to be a relationship between long lies and prospective poorer
21
22   health outcomes, this still remains a poorly understood area of investigation. Reducing long-lies is an
23
24   important aspect of healthcare delivery with the potential to limit avoidable harms and the associated
25
26
     morbidity and mortality concomitant with falling in the older person. However, the most prominent
27   finding in this research is the lack of available evidence surrounding the relationship between a non-
28
29   traumatic fall in the older person resulting in a long lie and poorer physical and clinical health
30
31   outcomes. This is partly due to a lack of robust evidence demonstrating the ill-effects of a long lie but
32
33   also due to difficulties capturing details pertaining long lie such as the length of time between a fall
34   and a call for help, which can be difficult to obtain through patient recall. This finding points to the
35
36   need for the exploration into newer technologies to overcome the need for the individual to
37
38   personally trigger fall alarms, as well as qualitative research to examine the lived experience of fall
39
40   technology and experiencing a fall resulting in a long lie. The emotional consequences associated with
41
42
     falling in older adults should also be acknowledged, and a fear of falling or experiencing a fall in the
43   absence of traumatic injury, can result in reduced self-confidence and a reluctance to engage in
44
45   everyday activities 24 and can place increased demands on caregivers for support. 25 Exploration and
46
47   analysis of big datasets to better understand and identify health outcomes of fall victims could also
48
49   increase understanding of the future complications and long-term outcomes of experiencing a long lie
50   and contribute towards the currently limited evidence base in this important area of research.
51
52
## 53   Given the increasing number of older people living alone or with the support from carers, examining
54
55   the lived experience of a long lie, potential future complications and views and experiences of using
56
     personal alarms, could provide valuable insights in how to support this vulnerable patient group.
57
58
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
     Future research should also focus on evolving interventions for wearable, smart and e-technology for
 1
 2   automatic fall detection. Indeed, Wang et al. 26 provide a comprehensive review of fall detection
 3
     systems which are designed to either detect or predict a fall and encompass a range of wearable,
 4
 5   visual, ambient and sensor fusion systems. Whilst the authors state a combination of visual and
 6
 7   wearable devices offer the most accurate method of fall detection, additional research is required for
 8
 9   accurate benchmarking between different interventions. Such research may overcome some of the
10
11   limitations associated with personal alarm use and enable strategies for reducing the potential
12   negative consequences of suffering a fall in the community setting with an associated long lie.
13
14
15
## 16   Limitations
17
## 18   Experiencing a long lie fall in the older person is an important area of concern in healthcare, however
19
20   a reliance on patient or carer recall to determine the length of a long lie means that obtaining an
21   accurate measure of the physical and clinical health outcomes of long lie in the older person is difficult
22
23   to quantify. These inconsistencies can lead to inaccurate reporting and measuring of the long-term
24
25   implications of a long lie fall. Moreover, the papers included in this review had an overreliance on the
26
27   use of personal alarms in preventing long lies in the older person, rather than a specific focus on the
28
     long-term outcomes associated with a long lie fall, perhaps attributable to the lack of robust clinical
29
30   evidence. Thus, the physical implications of being unable to get up from the floor following a non-
31
32   traumatic fall and the potential threat of pressure related damage or injury is difficult to understand.
33
## 34   This is important, as the potential of developing a pressure related injury due to limited mobility is
35
36   greatly increased in the older person population. Although all the studies reported in this review
37   defined a long lie as being unable to get up from the floor for <60 minutes following a fall, a lack of a
38
39   formal definition of a long lie means that some studies could have been excluded from this review.
40
## 41   Only 4 cohort studies were included in this review therefore there could be a potential for risk of bias
42
43   in the findings. However, utilising risk of bias interpretation tool for cohort studies, this review
44
     reported a low risk of bias and this points to the need for more research into the experiences of a long
45
46   lie fall in the older person. Future research should focus on a consensus for defining long lie fall and
47
48   developing robust methodologies for accurately determining the physical and clinical impacts of long
49
50   lie in the older person.

## Abstract
   3
   4
## 5          Background: The long term impacts of experiencing a ‘long lie’ following a fall in the older person
   6          are poorly understood. This systematic review explored the impact of a long lie fall on physical and
   7
   8          clinical outcomes in older people requiring an ambulance.
   9
  10
  11          Methods: PRISMA guidelines were followed.
  12
  13
## 14          Results: 70 studies were identified. Nine studies were suitable for full review. Four studies meeting
  15
  16          the inclusion criteria were included. The Critical Appraisal Skill Programme (CASP) assessed the
  17          quality of all included studies. Three studies reported on people aged 65 years and older. One study
  18
  19          reported on people aged over 90 years. Personal alarm use was examined in two studies. One study
  20
  21          explored patient characteristics of people conﬁrmed to have fallen by paramedics at the scene. One
  22
  23          study examined re-contact and characteristics of fallers referred to a falls prevention service.
  24
  25
## 26          Discussion: Cognitive impairment and long lie were a caveat for falls and repeated falls. Personal
  27          alarm use was infrequent suggesting a need for supporting the older patient in appropriate alarm
  28
  29          use and exploration of newer technologies to alleviate their need. Future research should focus on
  30
  31          interventions for wearable, smart and e-technology for automatic fall detection and qualitative
  32
  33          exploration of the lived experience.

## 3   Introduction
 4
 5   Falls are a common occurrence and are the largest cause of hospital admission in the older person;
 6
 7   between 2017-2018 approximately 220,160 emergency hospital admissions in the United Kingdom
 8   (UK) were attributable to falls in this patient group (aged 65 years and over) with just over 66% of
 9
10   these patients aged over 80 years. 1 Hospital-related falls account for approximately 240,000
11
12   reported in-patient safety incidents in UK hospitals 2, with an estimated cost of 2.3 billion. 3 A fall is
13
14   defined as an event which causes a person to, unintentionally, rest on the ground or other lower
15
16
     level. For the purposes of this paper, a non-traumatic fall is defined as a fall not causing significant
17   trauma or injury to the patient. 4
18
19
## 20   The prevention and management of falls in older people have been the focus of UK national
21
22   government initiatives which have endeavoured to focus on the reduction of morbidity and mortality
23   in this patient group. 5, 6 The ambulance service is under growing pressure to provide care provision to
24
25   an increasing number of older people who fall in their own home 7; a frequent reason for an
26
27   emergency ambulance call. 8 Even in those people who do not necessitate hospital admission, there
28
29   is an increased likelihood of poorer health outcomes, an increased risk of future admission to hospital
30
     and increased morbidity than the general older person population. 9 Community related falls are
31
32   largely preventable, however, data suggests that approximately 1 in 3 adults over 65 will experience
33
34   one fall annually 3 and an estimated 5% of falls in the community result in hospitalisation for injuries
35
36   such as fractures. 10 Snooks et al 11 found that 8% of all calls made to the London Ambulance Service
37
38   were due to older people who had fallen at home, with 36% of these people not requiring an
39   admission to hospital, despite 49% of fallers requiring healthcare assistance in the two-weeks post-
40
41   fall, with an increased risk of death and hospital admission than the general population.
42
43
## 44   Clinical and physical age-related complications such as muscle weakness, visual impairment or loss of
45   balance, as well as environmental factors such as a hazardous environment increase fall-related risk.
46
## 47   Hazards in the home resulting in a fall cost the NHS in the UK approximately £435 million 12 and
48
49   adapting the home environment to improve safety and limit fall-risk has been a focus of interventions
50
51   in falls prevention. 13 Suffering a fall at home can be a precursor to further future falls and the
52
     rehabilitative resources required to manage an older person post-fall can place significant demands
53
54   on healthcare services. 3
55
56
## 57   Experiencing a long lie fall
58
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
     A potentially serious complication of community-related falls in older people is the inability to get up
 1
 2   following a fall, which is associated with severe psychological, emotional and physical distress
 3
     connected to a fear of future falls, pressure-related injuries and loss of mobility. 3 Providing
 4
 5   information on how to avoid a ‘long lie’, generally defined as being unable to get up from the floor for
 6
 7   over one hour following a fall, is a priority in the NICE recommendations for preventing falls in older
 8
 9   people. 3 However, there is scant evidence surrounding the long-term outcomes of people who
10
11   experience a long lie fall in the community setting. Fleming et al 14 undertook a prospective cohort
12   study in the UK to examine the use of call alarm systems and their association with experiencing a long
13
14   lie in people over 90 years of age who had fallen at home. In a sample of 110 participants, at one-year
15
16   follow-up 60% were reported to have experienced a fall on at least one occasion and falls in the
17
18   community setting accounted for 45% of all reported falls. Interestingly, just over half of all reported
19
     falls resulted in the participant being found on the floor, with the majority of these participants
20
21   requiring assistance to get up from the floor. A total of 15% of all reported falls resulted in a long lie,
22
23   however, accounting for the individual people who fell rather than the reported number of falls, long
24
25   lies represented 30% of falls. Those who did experience a long lie were more likely to suffer
26
27   subsequent serious injuries, admissions to hospital and future long term care. Conversely, Simpson et
28   al 15 explored the characteristics of older people who had fallen in Australia and called an ambulance
29
30   through a prospective cohort study from a population of 1,610 cases and found that just over 53% of
31
32   patients were still on the ﬂoor when the ambulance arrived. Due to the quick ambulance response
33
34   time, only 13% of these patients experienced a “long-lie”. Cannon et al 16 found no relationship
35
     between ambulance response time and long lie in a sample of 503 patients.
36
37
## 38   The physical and clinical impacts on the older person experiencing a long lie remain poorly understood
39
40   with variations in the patient population investigated, study methodologies and a lack of robust clinical
41
42
     evidence contributing to a lack of understanding around the long term impacts of a long lie fall. As the
43   ambulance service are required to respond to an increasing number of older people who fall,
44
45   understanding the physical and clinical impacts of a fall is fundamental to limiting the number of
46
47   people suffering a long lie.
48
49
## 50   Aims and objectives
51
## 52   This systematic review aimed to explore the relationship between a non-traumatic fall in people aged
53
54   65 years or over resulting in a long lie (defined as remaining on the floor for an hour or longer) and
55
56   subsequently cared for by the ambulance service, and the associated physical and clinical health
57
58   related outcomes. The following objectives were defined to achieve this aim:
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
         1. To determine if there is an association between length of time spent on the floor after falling
 1
 2           and physical and clinical outcomes for older patients.
 3
 4       2. To understand whether the type and timeliness of response provided by the ambulance
 5
 6           service has any physical or clinical consequences for older patients who have a long-lie after
 7
 8           falling.

## Discussion
53
     This review synthesised evidence exploring the relationship between a non-traumatic fall in the
54
55   older person resulting in a long lie and poorer physical and clinical health outcomes. Limited
56
57   evidence was found surrounding the long term outcomes of long lies with only four cohort studies
58
59   meeting the inclusion criteria. Two studies specifically reported the effect of long lies on health
60
61
62
63
64
65
     outcomes. 14 19 The quality of all included studies was assessed using appropriate appraisal checklist
 1
 2   developed by the Critical Appraisal Skill Programme (CASP). 18 All four cohort studies were subjected
 3
     to quality assessment via risk of bias evaluation and had a low or very low risk of bias with all studies
 4
 5   focusing on long lie as an outcome.
 6
 7
 8
 9
## 10   All studies reported that the majority of falls in the community occurred in the home setting, with the
11
12   person predominantly being found alone when the ambulance service arrived. Only a small number
13
14   of ambulance calls following a fall resulted in a long lie, attributed to the rapid response times of the
15
16   ambulance service, which prevented the manifestation of fall-related complications. Transport to
17   hospital for fall-related injuries were relatively uncommon, which is consistent with previous research.
18
     11
## 19        All included studies suffered from the methodological limitation of a reliance on faller recall or
20
21   clinical documentation to determine the amount of time the person had been on the floor prior to
22
23   calling the ambulance, meaning it is difficult to establish the exact length of time between
24
     experiencing a fall and receiving assistance.
25
26
27   Whilst a significant proportion of calls to the ambulance do not result in traumatic injury to the patient,
28
29   the evidence suggests that there is a relationship between falls in the community setting and
30
     increased future morbidity, with many people necessitating future fall-related healthcare assistance
31
32   and support. Indeed, Flemming and Brayne 14 found that serious injury was consistently associated
33
34   with lying on the floor above one hour with 60% of those who were found on the floor following
35
36   ambulance assistance subsequently admitted to hospital with a fall-related issue. Furthermore, in this
37
38   study, long lies were associated with subsequent falls and future moves into long-term care
39   demonstrating the potential long-term future health implications that experiencing a long lie may
40
41   entail. Scott 20 also found that patients experiencing a long lie were also more likely to require future
42
43   fall-related ambulance assistance.
44
## 45   Although not a focus of this review, the finding that personal alarm use was minimal in two studies is
46
47   an interesting area of exploration. The reluctance to activate an alarm call from fallers found by
48
## 49   Flemming and Brayne 14 could be consistent with evidence demonstrating that the non-use of alarms
50
51   is associated with being unable to access the alarm, perceived patient burden and potential
52
53
     consequences of alarm activation. 19 Although alarm use should not necessarily be considered a
54   preventative measure for falling or long lie 14 some evidence points towards their value in supporting
55
56   people who experience a fall and require help. 21 It appears that the hesitancy of alarm use is
57
58   concomitant with fear and anxiety around their usage and suggests that there is a need for teaching
59
60   and support for the older patient in activating the alarm and supporting older people through
61
62
63
64
65
     education on the effective and appropriate use of personal alarm systems could prove valuable in
 1
 2   limiting the effects of experiencing a long lie. Newer technologies to alleviate the need for falls victims
 3
     to personally trigger alarms, or the potential for big dataset interrogations to better describe health
 4
 5   outcomes.
 6
 7
## 8   Future complications as a result of experiencing a long lie is not a new phenomenon; Wild et al. 22
 9
10
     found that older people experiencing a long lie were five times more likely to die within one year than
11   age and sex matched controls, indicative of the vulnerability a long lie can have on ill-health. In a
12
13   community based cohort study examining the predictors and prognosis associated with the inability
14
15   to get up following a fall in people aged 72 years and over, Tinetti et al. 23 found that being unable to
16
17   get up was associated with an increased risk of future hospitalisation, reduced mobility and an
18   increased risk of mortality than those fallers who could get up, although these findings were not
19
20   significant. Although there appears to be a relationship between long lies and prospective poorer
21
22   health outcomes, this still remains a poorly understood area of investigation. Reducing long-lies is an
23
24   important aspect of healthcare delivery with the potential to limit avoidable harms and the associated
25
26
     morbidity and mortality concomitant with falling in the older person. However, the most prominent
27   finding in this research is the lack of available evidence surrounding the relationship between a non-
28
29   traumatic fall in the older person resulting in a long lie and poorer physical and clinical health
30
31   outcomes. This is partly due to a lack of robust evidence demonstrating the ill-effects of a long lie but
32
33   also due to difficulties capturing details pertaining long lie such as the length of time between a fall
34   and a call for help, which can be difficult to obtain through patient recall. This finding points to the
35
36   need for the exploration into newer technologies to overcome the need for the individual to
37
38   personally trigger fall alarms, as well as qualitative research to examine the lived experience of fall
39
40   technology and experiencing a fall resulting in a long lie. The emotional consequences associated with
41
42
     falling in older adults should also be acknowledged, and a fear of falling or experiencing a fall in the
43   absence of traumatic injury, can result in reduced self-confidence and a reluctance to engage in
44
45   everyday activities 24 and can place increased demands on caregivers for support. 25 Exploration and
46
47   analysis of big datasets to better understand and identify health outcomes of fall victims could also
48
49   increase understanding of the future complications and long-term outcomes of experiencing a long lie
50   and contribute towards the currently limited evidence base in this important area of research.
51
52
## 53   Given the increasing number of older people living alone or with the support from carers, examining
54
55   the lived experience of a long lie, potential future complications and views and experiences of using
56
     personal alarms, could provide valuable insights in how to support this vulnerable patient group.
57
58
59
60
61
62
63
64
65
     Future research should also focus on evolving interventions for wearable, smart and e-technology for
 1
 2   automatic fall detection. Indeed, Wang et al. 26 provide a comprehensive review of fall detection
 3
     systems which are designed to either detect or predict a fall and encompass a range of wearable,
 4
 5   visual, ambient and sensor fusion systems. Whilst the authors state a combination of visual and
 6
 7   wearable devices offer the most accurate method of fall detection, additional research is required for
 8
 9   accurate benchmarking between different interventions. Such research may overcome some of the
10
11   limitations associated with personal alarm use and enable strategies for reducing the potential
12   negative consequences of suffering a fall in the community setting with an associated long lie.
13
14
15
## 16   Limitations
17
## 18   Experiencing a long lie fall in the older person is an important area of concern in healthcare, however
19
20   a reliance on patient or carer recall to determine the length of a long lie means that obtaining an
21   accurate measure of the physical and clinical health outcomes of long lie in the older person is difficult
22
23   to quantify. These inconsistencies can lead to inaccurate reporting and measuring of the long-term
24
25   implications of a long lie fall. Moreover, the papers included in this review had an overreliance on the
26
27   use of personal alarms in preventing long lies in the older person, rather than a specific focus on the
28
     long-term outcomes associated with a long lie fall, perhaps attributable to the lack of robust clinical
29
30   evidence. Thus, the physical implications of being unable to get up from the floor following a non-
31
32   traumatic fall and the potential threat of pressure related damage or injury is difficult to understand.
33
## 34   This is important, as the potential of developing a pressure related injury due to limited mobility is
35
36   greatly increased in the older person population. Although all the studies reported in this review
37   defined a long lie as being unable to get up from the floor for <60 minutes following a fall, a lack of a
38
39   formal definition of a long lie means that some studies could have been excluded from this review.
40
## 41   Only 4 cohort studies were included in this review therefore there could be a potential for risk of bias
42
43   in the findings. However, utilising risk of bias interpretation tool for cohort studies, this review
44
     reported a low risk of bias and this points to the need for more research into the experiences of a long
45
46   lie fall in the older person. Future research should focus on a consensus for defining long lie fall and
47
48   developing robust methodologies for accurately determining the physical and clinical impacts of long
49
50   lie in the older person.
